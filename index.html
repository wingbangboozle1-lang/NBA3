Executive summary

This build originates its own NBA lines from published team ratings, prices them against a live market snapshot, measures the correlations a parlay would depend on, screens its own signals for the two failure modes that matter most (injury blindness and over-confident divergence), and ranks strategies on a luck-adjusted score rather than on wins. Everything below is traceable to a stored source; anything that could not be confirmed is left blank and listed on the Flagged for review page.

The honest headline

Every single modelled signal came out positive-EV. That is a red flag, not a result. The model ignores injuries; the market prices them. So wherever a team is missing rotation players, the model disagrees with the market by construction, and the disagreement looks like edge. Every modelled signal came out positive-EV against one book. That is not evidence of 20 bets worth taking; it is evidence that a model which ignores injuries will disagree with a market that prices them. The screens above exist to throw most of those disagreements away.

What was built

  • Originated lines for all 23 games in the window — modelled spread, total, expected possessions, and win probability for every game.
  • Signals and picks with a two-stage screen, expected value per unit, and the exact reason each one was kept or thrown away.
  • Player props and correlation — including measured correlation coefficients with 95% confidence intervals, and an explicit statement of which correlations could not be measured.
  • Six strategies scored against seven validation gates, ranked on a leaderboard that discounts luck.
  • Injury and rotation analysis from the league's own 5:00 PM report, paired with observed market movement.
  • Decision questions — what we know, what we do not, and how to find out.

Forecast skill and the luck adjustment

The one strategy with a genuinely computed score is win-probability calibration, because it needs no price history. It is scored with the Brier rule, the standard proper scoring rule for probability forecasts.

Read this before believing the score. In-sample diagnostic. Ratings cover the same window as the games being scored, so this overstates any real edge. It is published to show the arithmetic is internally consistent, not as a performance claim. The luck test says the gap between observed and expected wins is well inside what chance produces (p = 0.429), so the leaderboard discounts skill by that p-value instead of crediting the raw score.

Limits you should hold us to

What this build cannot do

  • It cannot claim profit. No historical closing-line archive was retrieved, so no ROI, no backtested return, no "units won" figure appears anywhere.
  • It cannot price player props. Props are quoted and de-vigged, but there is no minutes distribution, so no prop model probability is published. Those cells are blank and flagged.
  • It does not adjust for injuries in the line. The number is a pure team-rating number; the official availability list is a separate column.
  • One book, one snapshot. Prices come from a single provider, so "edge" means edge against that provider at that minute, not against the market.

What it does do properly

  • Measured, not asserted. The home edge and both distribution widths are computed from the game sample, with confidence intervals shown.
  • Two-sided prices. Every market is de-vigged before any comparison, so the book's margin is removed rather than counted as edge.
  • Correlations measured with error bars. A coefficient without a confidence interval is not evidence, so none is published without one.
  • Sources on every row. Each cell group links to the page it came from and is tagged official, secondary, derived, or unverified.
