Strategy leaderboard

The competition the brief asks for. Every rule below selects real games from the 140 out-of-sample forecasts, is scored against nine gates, and is ranked by a composite skill score. Reward tiers decide what a rule is allowed to risk.

What a rule is actually tested against. Not a bookmaker — our own line. Because our line is originated to be fair, a rule that wins more than half the time against it is evidence that our line is biased in the games the rule picks. That is a model defect to repair, not an edge to bet (see CQ-40). This is the only question the data can answer without a licensed price feed.

The luck threshold. A board of 15 rules will throw up one impressive-looking result by chance. Simulating the whole board under the fair-line null 5,000 times, the best edge anywhere on it reaches 28.9 percentage points 5% of the time. The largest edge any real rule posted is 10.7 pp. Nothing clears the bar. Gate G9 is the formal version of this test and no rule passes it.

3. Mirror pairs

Six of the rules are exact complements of another rule: same games, opposite side. If one wins 68% the other wins 32%. They are one finding, not two, and a board of 15 rows is therefore closer to nine independent pieces of evidence. Both directions are published so the loser is visible — hiding it is how a backtest flatters itself — but only the winning direction can carry a score.

4. The control

“Back the home team in every game” is scored on the same 140 forecasts as everything else. It posts 48.6% against a 50% fair rate (p = 0.8), an edge of -1.4 pp, and fails 5 of 9 gates. That is the result we want. If the null control passed, the gates would be broken and every other verdict on this page would be worthless.
